Research

I use numerical models, observations, and theory to understand the physical processes that transform and transport water masses in high-latitude oceans.

Antarctic Coastal Polynyas

Antarctic coastal polynyas are sites of intense sea-ice formation, brine rejection, deep winter mixing, and rapid springtime restratification. My Ph.D. work used coupled ice-shelf, sea-ice, and ocean simulations, together with satellite and in situ observations and analytical scaling, to identify the physical controls on these seasonal transitions.

The winter studies quantify how offshore and alongshore winds, atmospheric cooling, background stratification, and coastal geometry regulate the time scale of water-column destratification and the export of dense shelf water. The spring study examines how sea-ice meltwater, ice-shelf basal meltwater, runoff, and wind-driven circulation establish near-surface stratification.

Dense Overflows Across the Iceland–Faroe Ridge

Dense waters crossing the Iceland–Faroe Ridge provide a major pathway linking the Nordic Seas to the deep North Atlantic. My postdoctoral work at Johns Hopkins investigates how time-dependent circulation affects the pathways and export of dense water across the Iceland–Faroe Ridge.

I use Lagrangian particle trajectories embedded in high-resolution model circulation to separate direct overflow, recirculation, and alternative export pathways, and to diagnose how subinertial variability modifies cross-ridge exchange. The current manuscript examines how subinertial variability alters overflow transport, pathway partitioning, dispersion, and downstream export.

Greenland Fjord Dynamics

Calving events can generate short, intense stresses within ice mélange and produce rapid fjord responses. I use idealized process models to study how these transient events alter horizontal circulation, secondary overturning, meltwater-plume pathways, and mixing.

This work is designed to isolate the dynamical response to short-duration forcing and connect transient fjord circulation to changes in stratification and water-mass exchange.

Next direction: Ocean Ventilation and Oxygen

At Georgia Tech, I will work in Takamitsu Ito’s lab to extend this process-oriented perspective toward large-scale ocean ventilation and oxygen variability, linking water-mass pathways and physical transport to climate-relevant biogeochemical change.
